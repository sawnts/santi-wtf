title: keep going
description: three months of running, an injury, and what starting over taught me about consistency and perfectionism.
date: 2026-08-31

one sunday morning in may, i woke up manic and mildly hungover, itching for a change. the day was hot, but i wasn’t gonna let a lil’ sunshine fuck up my day.

after kissing instagram good morning, my health app asked for some attention: *there’s been a change in your average daily step count*, she said. 3,000 and some change.

pathetic. my day was officially fucked.

there’s a walking pad in my closet covered in two years’ worth of dust. i bought it back when i thought averaging 5,000 steps a day was bad, and i’ve used it maybe six times. walking while working does serious damage to my aura, but i digress.

that morning, my health app helped me connect a few dots: the 30 extra pounds, the aches covering my body, and the fog clouding my days all traced back to the same thing. i wasn’t moving.

i have a demanding day job, a few hobbies, and a handful of friends i love dearly but leave on read way longer than they deserve. where was i going to find 90 minutes for the other 7,000 steps?

a sick idea popped into my head. to this day, i’m convinced it was the rational thing to do, and yet something tells me i might have lost my mind.

## i started running

i drove to best buy and bought a fancy watch. then i hit my local running store, got my feet scanned, and grabbed a pair of shoes. $500 later, i loaded a free 5k plan onto my garmin, laced up my new balances, and gave my baseline run — a single mile — everything i had. it was 10 minutes of hell. by the end, i could taste blood in my lungs, but i had no breath left to complain.

that was three months ago.

## a lot can happen in three months

my idea of consistency has shifted. more accurately, i’m finally learning what consistency is not. i thought it meant showing up perfectly. it means showing up reliably — again and again, imperfectly.

i’m a perfectionist. i raise the stakes of everything i do to unreachable heights. it’s the perfect hiding spot for my fears: if the expectations are impossible to meet, quitting — or worse, never starting — becomes not only acceptable but guaranteed.

perfectionism is pretentious procrastination.

there’s no perfect in running. no perfect form, distance, or pace. there’s only you — gasping for air, in pain, imperfect. when i run, there’s no hiding. pushing past my limits leaves me nowhere to go but toward a more honest version of me.

## knowing when to pull back

but running isn’t only about pushing limits. you also have to know when to pull back, and i learned that the hard way.

i got overexcited my first month and went from 3,000 steps a day to 10–15 miles a week. at 250 pounds, before i even knew how to run, that’s a very silly thing to do. i picked up a calf injury almost immediately, and between the injury and some travel, i had to take time off.

when i finally laced up again, all my gains were gone. it was crushing. the injury wasn’t serious, but it was serious enough to set me back and make the perfectionist in me want to quit.

so i’m changing my approach. in year one, my focus is learning to run, learning to stay healthy, and learning to think in years. i used to go balls to the wall at everything. that got me where i am today, but it won’t get me where i’m going next. i don’t want the fastest route anymore. i want the scenic one. i’m not obsessing over pace. i’m obsessing over consistency across a long-enough timeline.

it still blows my mind that i’m running 15–20 miles a week. it’s not a crazy number — some homies clock 50-plus — but for a civilian who grew up hating running, it’s pretty damn good. i’m proud of myself for finally finding an addiction that makes my life better.

running used to be punishment for what my body couldn’t do. now it’s a celebration of what it can.

## one foot in front of the other

the perfectionist doesn’t only show up on runs. for almost two years, i wrote a letter every week. when the streak started to slip, he decided that showing up late was worse than not showing up at all, so i disappeared for three months. this is me showing up late.

running has taught me that perfect is an illusion we manufacture to avoid the dirty work. it doesn’t matter how you start, where you start, or when you start — as long as you start, and you keep going.

one foot in front of the other.
